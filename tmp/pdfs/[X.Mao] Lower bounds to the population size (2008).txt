            LOWER BOUNDS TO THE POPULATION SIZE WHEN CAPTURE
                   PROBABILITIES VARY OVER INDIVIDUALS

                                               CHANG XUAN MAO
                                              University of California

                                                       Summary
       The problem of estimating population sizes has a wide range of applications. Although the
       size is non-identifiable when a population is heterogeneous, it is often useful to estimate
       the lower bounds and to construct lower confidence limits. A sequence of lower bounds,
       including the well-known Chao lower bound, is proposed. The bounds have closed-form
       expressions and are estimated by the method of moments or by maximum likelihood. Real
       examples from epidemiology, wildlife management and ecology are investigated. Simulation
       studies are used to assess the proposed estimators.
       Key words : capture–recapture; one-sided inference.

                                                   1. Introduction
        Consider a population consisting of N individuals. There are t capture periods. Let n x
be the number of individuals captured x times, x = 0, 1, . . . , t. The number of observed
                              
individuals is n = tx=1 n x = N − n 0 . We wish to estimate the population size N based on
n 1 , n 2 , . . . , n t . This problem has a wide range of applications (Chao, 2001), three of which are
shown in Table 1. The first (hav ) concerns college students infected by the hepatitis A virus
and identified from three sources (Chao et al., 2001); the second (vole ) concerns meadow
voles captured over five days (Pollock et al., 1990; Lee & Chao, 1994); and the third (ant )
concerns ant species found at 41 sites (Mao, Colwell & Chang, 2005).
        The problem has been thoroughly investigated in the literature. A binomial mixture
model is usually adopted (e.g. Burnham & Overton, 1978; Chao, 1989; Norris & Pollock,
1996; Pledger, 2000; Mao & Lindsay, 2002; Dorazio & Royle, 2003; Mao et al., 2005). There
are, however, alternative models (e.g. Lee & Chao, 1994).
        Link (2003) discussed the non-identifiability of the population size N . Dorazio & Royle
(2003) observed that two models can give competing fits to the observed counts yet yield
dramatically different estimates for N . Mao & Lindsay (2004) concluded that a confidence
interval for N must have a certain probability of having infinite upper limit. Although it is
impossible to estimate a non-identifiable parameter, it is often useful to estimate its bounds,
if such bounds exist. Chao (1989) considered estimating a lower bound of the population
size N . Chao, Shen & Hwang (2006) found that, by focusing on individuals whose capture
probabilities are bounded from below, lower bounds can be constructed in several different
settings. We will generalize the result in Chao (1989) by constructing a sequence of lower
bounds of N . These lower bounds can be estimated by the method of moments or by maximum

                                                      4. Discussion

      Discrete mixing distributions are appropriate for most applications. Sometimes con-
tinuous ones are used for modelling convenience. Although we have considered discrete
distributions, our results can be applied to continuous ones.
      For a heterogeneous population, it is possible to develop similar lower bounds in the
cases for which there are behavioural responses or for which there are time effects. In both
cases, there are mixtures of univariate discrete densities that admit moment structures (Norris
& Pollock, 1996; Mao, 2007).
      The lower bound of the population size N in Chao (1989) was shown to be the first one
in a sequence of lower bounds. These lower bounds retain the simplicity of the lower bound
in Chao (1989) and can be estimated easily. They can be useful if one wishes to construct
lower confidence limits for N .
      The distribution of a lower bound estimator often has a heavy right tail. The sample mean
can be huge. One may wish to reduce its estimation bias. In particular, an adjusted version of
