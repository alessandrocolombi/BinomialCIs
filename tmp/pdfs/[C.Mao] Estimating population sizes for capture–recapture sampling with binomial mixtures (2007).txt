Abstract
   A binomial mixture model has been used to account for heterogeneous individual capture probabilities in capture–recapture
studies. The problem of estimating the population size is reduced to estimating the odds that an individual is not captured. The odds
is nonidentiﬁable. There is no upper bound to the odds. The odds admits lower bounds that can yield estimable lower bounds to the
population size. An example is studied. A simulation experiment is used to evaluate the proposed and existing estimators.

Keywords: Capture–recapture; Nonidentiﬁability; Species richness




1. Introduction

   The problem of estimating the population size has enormous important applications in wildlife management, epi-
demiology, quality control and many other ﬁelds (e.g., Chao, 2001). A variety of estimators have been proposed.
Because a population is usually heterogeneous in the sense that the capture probabilities can vary over several factors,
extreme difﬁculties have been recognized (e.g., Link, 2003).
   There is one important setting: capture–recapture sampling with heterogeneous individual capture probabilities (e.g.,
Burnham and Overton, 1978; Norris and Pollock, 1996; Mao and Lindsay, 2002). A binomial mixture model arises
when the capture probabilities are treated as a random sample from a mixing distribution. In Section 2, the problem
of estimating the population size is reduced to estimating the odds that an individual is not captured. The odds is
nonidentiﬁable so that identiﬁable bounds are of interest. While the odds does not admit upper bounds, lower bounds
are available. A sequence of lower bounds, including the sharpest one, are proposed, which can be calculated by linear
programming. In Section 3, an example is studied. In Section 4, simulation is used to evaluate the proposed and existing
estimators. Technical points are discussed in the Appendix.

5. Discussion

   The issue of nonidentiﬁability does not arise given a parametric mixture model from a family of continuous mixing
distributions such as beta distributions or discrete ones such as those have at most two support points. However, it
is often difﬁcult to justify why a particular family of mixing distribution is chosen. Without prior information on
mixing distributions, the nonparametric mixture model is the most appropriate choice, in which the population size is
nonidentiﬁable.
   Link (2003) concluded that “the examples strongly call into question the usefulness of nonparametric methods; they
simply cannot be reliable in all cases”. It is not necessary to be so pessimistic. The proposed lower bounds to the
population size are useful in the sense that they provide sensible partial information and their estimators yield lower
conﬁdence limits for the population size when genuine upper conﬁdence limits do not exist (Mao and Lindsay, 2006).
   Although the appropriate choice of the order of the proposed lower bounds depends on particular applications, there
are some recommendations. When t is small, there are only a few lower bounds and the sharpest one is recommended.
When t gets larger, although more lower bounds are available, as it is often difﬁcult to precisely and accurately estimate
higher order lower bounds, including the sharpest one, the second and third order ones are recommended.
