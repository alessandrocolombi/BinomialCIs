          Confidence intervals for maximum unseen
     probabilities, with application to sequential sampling
                             design
 1                                                    Abstract
 2             Discovery problems often require deciding whether additional sampling is needed to detect
 3         all categories whose prevalence exceeds a prespecified threshold. We study this question under a
 4         Bernoulli product (incidence) model, where categories are observed only through presence–absence
 5         across sampling units. Our inferential target is the maximum unseen probability, the largest
 6         prevalence among categories not yet observed. We develop nonasymptotic, distribution-free upper
 7         confidence bounds for this quantity in two regimes: bounded alphabets (finite and known number
 8         of categories) and unbounded alphabets (countably infinite under a mild summability condition).
 9         We characterise the limits of data-independent worst-case bounds, showing that in the unbounded
10         regime no nontrivial data-independent procedure can be uniformly valid. We then propose data-
11         dependent bounds in both regimes and establish matching lower bounds demonstrating their
12         near-optimality. We compare empirically the resulting procedures in both simulated and real
13         datasets. Finally, we use these bounds to construct sequential stopping rules with finite-sample
14         guarantees, and demonstrate robustness to contamination that introduces spurious low-prevalence
15         categories.

16   Keywords: Bernoulli product model; confidence intervals; large alphabet inference; maximum unseen
17   probability; missing mass; minimax lower bounds; sequential stopping; unseen species.



18   1     Introduction

19   1.1    Background and motivation

20   Missing probabilities, the probability mass assigned to as-yet-unobserved outcomes, are central in-

21   ferential objects in many domains, including ecology (Chao et al., 2014), optimization (Dick et al.,

22   2014), genomics (Ionita-Laza et al., 2009; Masoero et al., 2022), security analysis (Bubeck et al.,

23   2013), debugging (Chao et al., 1993), and language modeling (Noorani et al., 2025). A statistical

24   question in these settings is when to stop sampling: when does additional effort cease to be justified

25   by the information gained on rare or unobserved symbols? Stopping decisions are often guided by

26   coverage-based diagnostics, species-accumulation curves, or rules of thumb. For instance, in ecology

27   it is common to rely on coverage-based rarefaction and extrapolation to standardise sampling effort,

28   and to deem a sample “complete enough” once estimated coverage exceeds a fixed threshold or the

29   species-accumulation curve has flattened (Chao and Jost, 2012; Béguinot, 2015; Montes et al., 2021).

                                                          1
30   In clinical safety and reliability, zero-event rules argue that an unobserved adverse event must be

31   sufficiently rare (Hanley and Lippman-Hand, 1983). Similarly, in recapture debugging of computer

32   code, stopping rules balance the expected number of remaining bugs against testing cost (Chao et al.,

33   1993). In uncertainty quantification for language models, Noorani et al. (2025) asked how many times

34   a query should be repeated to observe all answers the model can generate, casting it as an optimal

35   stopping rule driven by missing probabilities.

36      Such stopping rules provide useful guidance in practice, but their justification is typically asymp-

37   totic or oracle-based, and their performance can be fragile in the presence of contamination mechanisms

38   that generate many artificial low-prevalence symbols. For example, in high-throughput sequencing,

39   PCR artefacts can produce large numbers of rare OTUs (operational taxonomic units), thereby in-

40   flating the apparent tail of the distribution (Schloss et al., 2011). Similarly, in metabarcoding studies,

41   barcode/tag misidentification and index swapping can misassign reads across samples, yielding many

42   artefactual taxa that appear only a handful of times (Eisenhofer et al., 2019).

43      In this paper, we focus on settings where the basic observation for each category is an incidence

44   indicator across sampling units (patients, sites, or samples), that is, for each symbol we only record

45   presence or absence within each unit. This setting arises naturally in several applications: in high-

46   throughput genomics and somatic mutation studies, data record whether a given mutation is present in

47   each patient (Ionita-Laza et al., 2009), with each patient presenting multiple mutations; in community

48   ecology and biodiversity monitoring, presence–absence matrices are a standard format for site-by-

49   species data (Magurran, 2004; Ghilotti et al., 2025; Stolf and Dunson, 2025); and in user-activity

50   modelling for online A/B testing, one records whether each user performs certain actions (Beraha

51   et al., 2025). A convenient formalisation is provided by a Bernoulli product model : for each symbol 𝑗 ∈

52   {1, . . . , 𝑀 } and each sampling unit 𝑖 ∈ {1, . . . , 𝑛}, we observe independent Bernoulli random variables

53   𝑋𝑖 𝑗 indicating presence or absence of symbol 𝑗 in unit 𝑖. The model is completed by assuming that

54   each symbol has an incidence probability 𝑝 𝑗 , constant across sampling units. We allow 𝑀 ≤ ∞, where

55   the extreme case 𝑀 = ∞ represents the possibility of countably many symbols; in the aforementioned


                                                          2
56   contexts, the underlying alphabet is large or effectively unbounded, and contamination can generate

57   many low-prevalence artefactual symbols.

67      The main contribution of this paper is a non-asymptotic, distribution-free analysis of confidence

68   intervals for the maximum unseen probability 𝑀max in the Bernoulli product model. We develop

69   a unified theory across two complementary regimes: a bounded-alphabet regime, where the total

70   number of categories 𝑀 is finite and known, and an unbounded-alphabet regime, where the alphabet

71   is countable and potentially infinite, under a mild summability condition on the prevalences.

72      In the bounded-alphabet regime, where 𝑀 is known, a multiplicity-adjusted rule of three suggests

73   that log(𝑀/𝛼)/𝑛 is a valid upper confidence bound for 𝑀max at level 1 − 𝛼. While appealingly simple,

74   this construction is conservative and does not exploit the realised pattern of observed and unobserved

75   categories. We first show how to improve upon this benchmark in a worst-case, data-independent

76   manner. We then introduce a data-dependent upper confidence bound that adapts to the empirical

77   incidence counts and yields a further tightening. Finally, we establish a matching lower bound, showing


                                                         3
78    that the resulting rate is minimax-optimal over bounded alphabets.

79       We then turn to unbounded alphabets under a mild summability assumption on the prevalences.

80    In this regime we first establish an impossibility result: no non-trivial worst-case (data-independent)

81    upper bound for 𝑀max can attain valid coverage uniformly over the model class. This motivates the

82    development of data-dependent procedures. We propose such an upper confidence bound, based on

83    a suitable smoothing of the maximum functional, and show that it is essentially tight by proving

84    a matching lower bound. A simulation study confirms the theoretical findings and suggests that the

85    unbounded-alphabet procedure can outperform the bounded-alphabet construction even when the true

86    alphabet is finite but contains many low-incidence symbols. Building on these experiments, we propose

87    a simple rule of thumb for choosing between the two intervals in practice.

88       Finally, we use our theory as building blocks for principled stopping rules in sequential sampling.

89    Given a prevalence threshold 𝜀 and confidence level 1 − 𝛼, we consider the procedure that stops at the

90    random time 𝜏 defined as the smallest sample size that guarantees that the probability of the maximum

91    unseen probability 𝑀max is smaller than 𝜀. such that the upper endpoint 𝑈𝑛 (𝛼) of one of our intervals

92    satisfies 𝑈𝑛 (𝛼) ≤ 𝜀. We evaluate the resulting stopping rules against coverage- and extrapolation-

93    based criteria from the ecological literature, under both clean and contaminated sampling scenarios in

94    which spurious low-prevalence categories are introduced to mimic sequencing and amplification errors.

95    The results indicate that our 𝑀max -based procedures achieve the desired risk control and remain

96    stable and robust under contamination, whereas coverage-based criteria may stop too early or require

97    substantially more sampling effort in the presence of many artefactual rare categories.



98    1.3    Related work

99    The closest work to ours is Painsky (2025), who studies confidence intervals for parameters of unob-

100   served events under multinomial sampling. He derives worst-case, dimension-free confidence bounds

101   for unseen-category probabilities, which can be used to control extremal quantities such as 𝑀max , with

102   applications to large-alphabet inference and ℓ∞ distribution estimation (Kontorovich and Painsky,

                                                        4
103   2025). Our focus is instead on the Bernoulli product (incidence) model, and on deriving tighter, data-

104   dependent bounds for 𝑀max in both bounded- and unbounded-alphabet regimes; we expect that some

105   of our techniques could also sharpen the worst-case multinomial bounds of Painsky (2025).

106      Stopping criteria based on coverage, richness estimators, and species-accumulation curves are

107   widely used in ecology. In particular, coverage-based rarefaction and extrapolation (Chao and Jost,

108   2012) are commonly employed to standardise effort and to assess whether sampling has become suf-

109   ficiently exhaustive; see also Béguinot (2015); Montes et al. (2021) and references therein. Related

110   approaches include Chao et al. (2009), who propose a method to estimate the additional sampling

111   effort required to achieve a fixed target fraction of the asymptotic richness.

112      Our work is also connected to the extensive literature on the missing mass (Good, 1953), and to

113   classical tools such as the Good–Turing estimator. Missing-mass ideas play a prominent role in se-

114   quential discovery and exploration problems, including modern applications to large language models

115   (Noorani et al., 2025), global optimization (Dick et al., 2014), and adaptive decision-making (Bubeck

116   et al., 2013). These contributions provide global diagnostics for unseen outcomes, but do not distin-

117   guish between many ultra-rare unobserved categories and a single moderately common unseen category.

118   Our analysis of 𝑀max targets precisely this extremal aspect and yields finite-sample, distribution-free

119   guarantees that translate directly into stopping rules.



120   1.4    Other applications

121   Although sequential stopping is a primary motivation, the confidence intervals for 𝑀max apply when-

122   ever one needs finite-sample control on the prevalence of unseen categories. A seminal discussion is

123   given by Hanley and Lippman-Hand (1983), who stress that observing zero events does not justify

124   concluding that the underlying risk is zero. This point is made concrete in the medical literature by

125   Eypasch et al. (1995), who advocate reporting upper confidence bounds for complications that have

126   not yet occurred. Such situations are common in clinical trials, diagnostic testing, and post-marketing

127   surveillance, where safety claims are often based on the absence of observed adverse events.

                                                         5
128       These concerns have motivated a substantial literature on confidence intervals and risk bounds

129   under zero or near-zero counts. In emergency medicine, Jovanovic and Zalenski (1997) emphasize

130   that standard normal approximations can fail when events are rare or unobserved, and advocate exact

131   binomial confidence intervals and simple rules of thumb such as the rule of three. In comparative

132   settings, Möller and Ahrenfeldt (2021) study inference for relative risks when one study arm exhibits

133   zero events, highlighting that valid inference is still possible but becomes delicate in small samples.

134       Closely related problems arise in reliability engineering, environmental risk assessment, and safety

135   certification, where large collections of components or failure modes are monitored and zero failures

136   are common. Quigley and Revie (2011) emphasize that maximum likelihood assigns probability zero

137   to unobserved failures, which is inadequate for risk-averse decision-making, and develop principled

138   alternatives. Across these domains, the common statistical structure is inference on many rare-event

139   probabilities for outcomes that do not appear in the sample, motivating finite-sample procedures that

140   account for multiplicity and provide meaningful guarantees for unobserved events.

324   4.2      Bounded vs unbounded bound: when to choose one over the other?

325   The experiments in Section 4.1 show that the Unbounded upper bound can yield shorter confidence

326   intervals than its Bounded counterpart, even when the alphabet size is known and finite. However,

327   this behavior is not universal: in several regimes the Unbounded bound performs poorly relative to

328   Bounded. Intuitively, the unbounded-alphabet assumption should be advantageous in heavy-tailed

329   settings, where many symbols remain undetected. In this section we propose empirical and heuristic

330   tools to guide the choice between the bounded and unbounded regimes. These tools are intentionally

331   simple and practical, providing fast guidance without requiring detailed tail modelling or power-law

332   analysis.

333      The first tool we propose is a simple visual inspection of the accumulation curve. Accumulation

334   curves, widely used in ecology (Colwell et al., 2009), plot the number of distinct species observed

                                                        16
335   as a function of sample size. Since the data are typically recorded without preserving the order

336   of appearance, the curve is usually obtained by averaging over multiple random permutations of

337   the sample. Such curves often exhibit rapid initial growth for small sample sizes, followed either by

338   stabilization around an apparent asymptote or by continued growth at a slower rate. Persistent growth

339   suggests that new symbols are still being discovered, consistent with many low-prevalence categories,

340   whereas a flattened curve indicates that most symbols have already been observed.

341      As a practical guideline, we recommend inspecting the accumulation curve of the data. If a clear

342   horizontal asymptote emerges, the Bounded upper bound should be preferred; otherwise, the Un-

343   bounded version is likely more appropriate. An illustrative example is provided in Figure 3.

380   4.3                  Application to the TCGA data

381   The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) is a large, publicly available cancer genomics resource that catalogs

382   somatic mutations from whole-exome sequencing data in tumor samples across 32 cancer types (Ellrott,

383   2018). These data are of major scientific importance, as identifying somatic mutations plays a central

384   role in precision oncology. However, while traditional analyses focus on well-known cancer genes with

385   relatively frequent mutations, large-scale sequencing studies have shown that somatic variants exhibit

386   a highly heterogeneous, long-tailed distribution: a small subset is repeatedly observed across tumors,

387   whereas most variants are extremely rare, often appearing only once or not at all.

388               Previous statistical analyses on the TCGA data (Chakraborty et al., 2019; Masoero et al., 2022;

389   Camerlenghi et al., 2024) have addressed the problem of estimating the number of new variants ex-


                                                                                 19
390   pected in future, unobserved tumor samples, based on information from existing patient cohorts. In

391   particular, Masoero et al. (2022); Camerlenghi et al. (2024) analyze each cancer type separated, and

392   model data for each cancer via a Bernoulli product model, where the different symbols in the alphabet

393   corresponds to the genomic variants. Even if such a model assumes independence across variants,

394   which is known to be violated in practice due to local correlations along the genome (Leiserson et al.,

395   2015), the predictions obtained are very accurate, see also Chakraborty et al. (2019); Masoero et al.

396   (2022) for discussion. Figure S5 in the appendix reports the sample size for each cancer type and the

397   corresponding number of discovered somatic variants .

398      While accurate predictions of this quantity can be highly valuable, for example, in inferring the

399   primary site of tumors of unknown origin or in interpreting evidence of a tumor detected in circu-

400   lating tumor DNA, here we take a complementary approach by addressing a related question: how

401   likely is it to encounter previously unseen variants? Our main contribution is to provide finite-sample,

402   distribution-free guarantees by constructing confidence intervals that simultaneously cover the preva-

403   lences of all unseen variants at a prescribed confidence level. This goes beyond estimating expected

404   discovery counts, and yields rigorous uncertainty quantification for rare and unobserved variants in

405   cancer genomic datasets.

406      To answer this question, we first determine whether the Bounded or the Unbounded upper bound

407   is more appropriate for the TCGA application. Both diagnostic tools in Section 4.2 provide consistent

515   5.3    Application to BBS dataset

516   The North American Breeding Bird Survey (BBS) is a continental monitoring program that has col-

517   lected standardized data on avian populations annually since 1966, and is a primary resource for

519   Surveys are conducted by trained volunteer observers along randomly selected roadside routes, each

520   consisting of 50 fixed stops spaced approximately 0.8 km apart. At each stop, observers perform a

522   resulting observations are collected under a highly standardized protocol and naturally yield presence–

523   absence information.

528   was accessed using the R package bbsAssistant (Burnett et al., 2019).

541   6                Discussion

542   The bounds derived in this paper are optimal in a minimax sense, as they match the lower bounds

543   we exhibit in Theorems 3 and 6. However, such lower bounds are attained by worst-case distributions

544   that erase most structural information. In many practical situations, the prevalence sequence is far

545   from least-favourable, and one should expect that substantially sharper uncertainty quantification is

546   possible by exploiting more of the observed incidence spectrum.

547                A natural direction, which we are currently pursuing, is to move from worst-case analysis to



                                                                    27
548   distribution-adaptive inference by introducing weak structural assumptions on the prevalence sequence.

549   One pragmatic route is to treat the prevalences as random variables and use a working model to lo-

550   calize inference in a Bayesian setting (either nonparametric or empirical). Developing such methods,

551   and understanding precisely when and how they improve over minimax procedures without sacrific-

552   ing robustness, seems especially promising in the unbounded setting, where uniform validity forces

553   conservative rates but typical instances may be far more benign.
